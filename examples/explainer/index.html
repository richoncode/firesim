How this fire works

Two stories, told in order. First, the fire you see. Then the sound this experiment adds beside it. No graphics card is required to read this page.

The fire is Dan Greenheck’s. Daniel Greenheck (dgreenheck) wrote Fire Pro, the real-time fire and smoke simulation this page describes. You can also open his editor. The project is MIT licensed. Copyright © 2026 Daniel Greenheck. This fork keeps that license, including his copyright notice.

About the sound experimenter

The sound on this page is Richard Bailey’s experiment on top of Fire Pro. It does not change how the fire is solved or drawn, except for optional dots that mark where the sound is listening.

Richard has long worked on game and spatial audio. He wrote the chapter “Spatial Emphasis of Game Audio: Creating a Theatrically Enhanced Audio Experience” in Audio Anecdotes III: Tools, Tips, and Techniques for Digital Audio, edited by Ken Greenebaum and Ronen Barzel (A K Peters, 2007). In it he presents methods he used building the Forza Motorsport audio engine: distorting the aural space, Doppler shifts, and other effects tied to game physics, to create a cinematic, hyperreal experience.

More of his work is on his portfolio.

A fun connection: Perry Cook, whose work on synthesized sound inspired the crackles here, wrote “Introduction to Physical Modeling” for the first Audio Anecdotes volume, the same series Richard later wrote for. Years before his Xbox spatial audio work, Richard read Cook’s book Real Sound Synthesis for Interactive Applications (A K Peters, 2002). What stuck with him was its idea of sampling energy at a few points and turning that into sound, and it’s why this experiment listens to the fire through a handful of probes.

How the fire simulation works

Fire Pro runs entirely on the graphics chip. Each frame it advances a blob of hot air, then draws it by walking a ray through that air for every pixel.

A grid that only exists where the fire is

The world is divided into little cubes called cells. Campfire cells are 5 cm on a side. The computer does not allocate a giant solid block of them. It groups cells into bricks (8, 16, or 32 cells along each side) and only gives a brick memory when an emitter, or flame, smoke, or fuel already there, needs it. Empty air costs nothing. The ground is a floor at height zero. Nothing is simulated below it. Solid objects block the flow.

Time moves in fixed bites of one sixtieth of a second. A frame takes at most one bite. If the frame was slower than that, the leftover time is dropped and the fire simply runs slow, instead of taking a huge jump.

What a cell remembers

Each flame cell holds four numbers. Heat here is a relative amount, not a thermometer reading in degrees. A separate grid can hold the wind, and the pressure used to keep that wind from piling air up. Wind and pressure may use coarser cells than the flame.

Smokehaze, or this step’s new smoke
Heathow hot, on the sim’s own scale
Flame lifehow much flame is left, from 0 to 1
Fuelunburned fuel. Never drawn.

When smoke uses a coarser grid, as the campfire does (smoke cells twice as wide), the haze you see lives on that coarser grid. The fine cell’s smoke slot then only stages the smoke produced this step.

One step, in the order the code actually runs it

“Advection” means carrying a value along the wind. “MacCormack” is a second pass that sharpens what that carry smeared. The wind itself is stored on the faces of each cell, not at the center, so flow into one face is flow out of the next. People call that a staggered, or MAC, grid.

  1. Make room. Decide which bricks are active. A brand-new source gets slots in this same step. Bricks that just appeared, and bricks that stopped computing, are cleared to still, empty air.
  2. Carry the wind, then sharpen it. Move the wind along itself. The path is a short curve (a two-stage trace). A bounded MacCormack pass then corrects the blur. Open air flowing in is still air, not a copy of whatever just left.
  3. Carry heat, smoke, flame, and fuel, then let them change. Scalars take the same kind of trip, with MacCormack on by default (it can be turned off, which leaves a blurrier first-order carry). Emitters and mesh sources add flame lifetime, smoke, heat, and fuel. If fuel is enabled and the cell is at least as hot as the ignition heat, fuel burns into flame — its amount, capped at 1, becomes the flame’s lifetime — and the gas expands as it burns. The flame’s own curve adds more heat, smoke, and expansion. Heat cools and smoke thins by a smooth exponential fade. A cell that was not just refilled, has no flame left, and has dropped below a tiny cutoff (0.001) is cleared.
  4. Update coarse smoke, when the scene uses it. The campfire does. That haze is carried and faded on its own coarser grid.
  5. Measure the swirl, if the scene asked for it. Swirl (vorticity) is how much the air is spinning. It is computed from the wind with central differences, and only when the vorticity setting is not zero. It is not a field the sim keeps around. A debug view that colors swirl computes it again just for the picture.
  6. Push. Forces are worked out at cell centers, then averaged onto the faces. Hot air rises: buoyancy times heat, upward. Smoke pulls downward. Vorticity confinement adds a small shove across the swirl so little eddies do not die out. Wind, turbulence, a vortex, or a radial push placed in the scene is added here too. The wind is then faded a bit (velocity damping) and this step’s push is added. Faces on a solid are set to zero. An emitter can also blend in a velocity it asked for.
  7. See where air is piling up. That pile-up is the divergence. The expansion the flame and the burning fuel already asked for is subtracted, so the pressure solve is allowed to keep that expansion.
  8. Solve for pressure. A few multigrid passes: a coarse version of the problem first, then a finer one. Each step starts from zero pressure rather than last frame’s answer.
  9. Project. Subtract the pressure change across each face. After this, the wind piles up only as much as the expansion allowed. Cells inside a solid are set to still.

After that, the sim copies a count of active bricks back for the on-screen stats. That count lags the picture slightly. The sound, described below, is a separate optional read. It is not one of these steps, and it never writes the fire.

How you see it

Fuel is stored but never drawn. A flame glows at full strength until the last fifth of its lifetime, then fades out with it. Smoke and flame both darken the ray. The glow uses that same darkness (Kirchhoff’s idea: a thing that emits also absorbs), so a thick flame blocks what is behind it instead of painting light on top of light.

Color comes from heat only at display time. The sim’s heat is turned into a color temperature: 800 kelvin plus a fraction of the way toward the temperature the artist set. The fraction is heat ÷ (heat + 2), so it levels off instead of climbing forever. That temperature is looked up in a blackbody table covering about 500 to 10,000 kelvin — the familiar dull red to white-hot ramp. An artist tint can replace the hue. White keeps the natural spectrum.

Smoke can scatter light, including light from the scene. The flame itself absorbs and emits. It does not scatter the room light. A separate pass turns the flame into lights for nearby surfaces. That lighting is only for the picture. It does not drive the sound.

How the sound extends it

The voice is a separate program on the audio clock (a Web Audio worklet). It never reads graphics memory itself. Playback hands it a small set of numbers after each simulated step. Until you click Unmute, or press U, the context is not even started, so the page is silent. Mute turns the output down to nothing and leaves the fire running.

The editor does not run this voice. The slider example has no fire grid either: its Level knob is the energy, all by itself, in mono. The campfire you hear on playback is the stereo version below.

A few listening posts

The sound does not listen to every cell, and it does not trace the flame front. It reads a handful of fixed probes, 4 to 32 of them. Each probe looks at one flame cell for heat, and at that spot’s wind cell plus its six neighbors for motion. Why probes? See About the sound experimenter.

  • Heat is that one cell’s heat.
  • Speed is the fastest wind in that little neighborhood, in meters per second.
  • Swirl is the spin in that same neighborhood, measured the same way as the vorticity debug view. The number is a rate per second.
  • Live means the brick actually has memory. An empty probe reports zeros.

The pass only reads. It does not write heat, wind, or anything else in the sim.

Three small read buffers

Copying numbers off the graphics chip can stall a frame if you wait for them. Playback does not wait. It copies the probe results into one of three map-read buffers and asks the browser to map that buffer later. The animation frame moves on. When the map finishes, a later frame picks the numbers up, so the voice is always at least one step behind the picture. If all three buffers are still busy, that step is skipped and the voice keeps the last values it heard. Changing the probe count throws those buffers away and starts clean, so an old map of the wrong size is ignored.

A short smooth, then a voice

Heat, speed, and swirl are eased with a filter about six hundredths of a second long, so one jumpy reading does not jab the speaker. The first reading is used immediately. Whether a probe is live is not smoothed. A sudden rise in the unsmoothed heat can still add a pop: at the default impulse setting, a rise of about 0.35 heat units is enough, and a bigger rise (toward 1.5 units) makes a bigger pop.

Playback is stereo. Each probe keeps its own crackle. One shared roar sits underneath, taken from the average energy of the probes that are actually burning. Quiet probes are left out of that average so empty air does not thin the bed. When a probe gets hotter, its energy approaches the new level at about 8 per second, so a plume that reaches the next probe still sounds like a new onset. It falls at the fire’s cooling rate, and never slower than 0.35 per second, so turning Level to zero still fades out. The fade uses the scene’s cooling setting plus a tenth of its smoke dissipation, from the CPU side, not a measurement inside each probe.

The roar is leaked noise through a low-pass filter. Hotter, more restless probes open the filter. Smoke darkens it. That smoke is a stand-in from how fast the scene is emitting smoke, shared by the whole voice, not a density read at the probe.

The crackle is inspired by Perry Cook’s PhISEM idea (random, Poisson-timed bursts). In this voice, collision times are a Poisson process, which means random pops with a chosen average rate, like raindrops. The rate grows with the square of the probe’s energy, so a quiet probe pops rarely and a hot one crackles. Each pop is a short burst of noise (the burst fades in about 20 ms) through three bandpass filters: a low one near 300 Hz, a middle one near 1.5–2.2 kHz, and a high one near 3.4–4.3 kHz. They brighten a little as energy rises. Cook wrote the idea up as Physically Informed Sonic Modeling (PhISM) in Computer Music Journal, 1997. His notes live on his Princeton page.

Letting a live fire drive a synthesizer, rather than playing a recording, is the spirit of Jeffrey Chadwick and Doug James, Animating Fire with Sound (SIGGRAPH 2011; ACM). This fork does not implement their combustion-sound model. The voice here is the crackle plus the filtered roar, driven by these few probes.

From a reading to a sound

For each live probe, after smoothing:

  • Loudness of that probe is its heat times heat gain, compared with 2 (a hot campfire cell, not the top of the heat debug scale, which runs 0–4), then multiplied by Level. If the sim has run out of brick memory, that result is multiplied by 0.55, matching cells the picture already dropped. Level 0 forces this, and any pop, to zero.
  • Motion mixes speed and swirl: 35% from speed (full at 4 m/s) and 65% from swirl (full at 50 per second). The Motion knob decides how much that mix matters. At 75%, a still probe keeps a quarter of its crackle rate, and motion raises the rest. At 100%, a still probe can go quiet.
  • Crackle rate starts from 70 pops a second at full energy, times the crackle-rate knob, times energy squared, times the motion factor above. The knob only goes up to 0.5. Campfire’s 0.05 means a fully hot probe crackles a few times a second, not a storm.
  • Pitch variation retunes that pop’s three filters. 0% keeps the centers fixed. 100% is up to an octave either way. The new pitch is chosen per pop.
  • Volume variation changes how hard each pop hits. 100% is the original spread, from a soft tap to a full hit. Lower pulls the hits toward the middle. Pops from a heat jump use a fixed hit, not this spread.
  • Roar mix crossfades the shared roar against all the crackles. The middle, 50%, keeps them in balance. Toward 0 you mostly hear crackle. Toward 100% you mostly hear roar.
  • Stereo. Each crackle pans from its own left-right position. About 60 cm of world X is a hard pan. The roar pans to the energy-weighted average of the burning probes, so a balanced fire stays centered.

More probes add more crackle. They are not turned down to compensate. Level is the master.

Where the probes sit

Neighbors are a chosen number of cells apart, at least 2, so they never share an edge. Each one sits on a cell center. The anchor is the fire: across the emitters, at the lowest emitter. Rebuilding the layout (count, spacing, or shape) forgets the smoother, so old cells do not bleed into the new ones.

Vertical One column on the fire, stepping up. Campfire starts here.
Horizontal One sheet at the emitter’s height. A prime count becomes a line across.
Box Extra probes go upward. Sixteen probes is 2×4×2. This is the tornado start.

The line in the dock names the shape. Campfire opens as “Vertical 1×11×1, 70 cells up”: eleven probes, seven cells apart, so seventy cells from the lowest to the highest (about 3.5 m on these 5 cm cells). Horizontal would say “on the emitter”. A box of sixteen probes at spacing 6, which is what Box and the tornado page use, reads “Box 2×4×2, 18 cells up”.

Odd box counts collapse to a single column. Even counts that are not a multiple of four are a vertical sheet, two probes wide. Horizontal splits the count into the closest rectangle that is at least as wide as it is deep.

The campfire controls

Campfire starts muted, on this ear-tuned setup. Reset on that page puts these back:

Level 23%. Vertical. 11 probes. Spacing 7. Heat gain 1.00×. Crackle rate 0.05×. Crackle pitch variation 82%. Crackle volume variation 78%. Roar mix 12% roar. Motion 75%. Impulse 1.00×. Probe markers on.

The tornado playback (?preset=tornado) uses the same dock with its own start: Level 70%, Box, 16 probes, spacing 6, crackle rate 0.50×, pitch variation 0%, volume variation 100%, roar mix 50%, motion 75%, impulse 1.00×, markers on. Reset there restores that set, not the campfire one.

  • Unmute / U. Starts the audio. The button becomes Mute, which cuts the output and leaves the fire running. The page is silent until that click or key.
  • Level. Master on every probe. Zero stops adding energy, and each crackle dies out at the cooling rate.
  • Probe layout, Probes, Spacing. Horizontal, Vertical, or Box; how many (4–32); how many cells apart (2–16). Any of these rebuilds the lattice. The shape line above them is the result.
  • Heat gain (0.25×–4×). Multiplies heat before comparing it with 2. 1.00× is unchanged.
  • Crackle rate (0–0.50×). Multiplies how often pops happen. It does not change the roar.
  • Crackle pitch variation and crackle volume variation. Per pop, as above. They do not retune the roar.
  • Roar mix. Crackle on the left of the slider, roar on the right. The label says “crackle”, “roar”, or a percent such as “12% roar”.
  • Motion. How much speed and swirl raise the crackle rate and brighten the roar. 75% is the campfire start.
  • Impulse (off, or up to 3×). How sensitive pops are to a sudden heat rise. Off disables them. The space bar still detonates bursts in the picture, but the voice does not take a separate pop from the key. Campfire has no burst emitters, so it stays on the probes.
  • Show probe positions. Draws a small sphere at each probe. They are markers only, not colliders and not fuel. Turn them off and the picture matches the original sim. They start on.
  • The energy strip under the knobs is what the probes last heard, after smoothing. On the vertical campfire, left is the emitter and right is the top. On a box, the bottom row is the emitter. On a horizontal sheet, each row is a line across the fire.
  • Reset restores the start listed above for the page you are on.
  • Log probe (or ?probe=1) prints a CPU-side summary and one heat line per completed map. That log is for tinkering. The voice is driven by the probe readings, not by that summary’s heat total.
